\checkin{01/15} Making the $u$-substitution $u= x^2 + 1$ in $\ds\int_0^1 \dfrac{5x}{x^2 + 1}\;dx$, we obtain $\ds\int_0^1 \dfrac{5}{2u} \;du$. \pspace

\sol The statement is \textit{false}. One must always remember to change the limits when making a substitution in a definite integral. If we choose $u= x^2 + 1$, then $du= 2x \;dx$. If $x= 0$, then $u= 0^2 + 1= 1$. If $x= 1$, then $u= 1^2 + 1= 2$. But then\dots
	\[
	\int_0^1 \dfrac{5x}{x^2 + 1}\;dx= 5 \int_0^1 \dfrac{x}{x^2 + 1}\;dx= \dfrac{5}{2} \int_0^1 \dfrac{2x}{x^2 + 1}\;dx= \dfrac{5}{2} \int_1^2 \dfrac{du}{u}
	\]
We also have\dots
	\[
	\dfrac{5}{2} \int_1^2 \dfrac{du}{u}= \dfrac{5}{2}\,\ln|u| \;\bigg|_1^2= \dfrac{5}{2}\; ( \ln 2 - \ln 1)= \dfrac{5}{2}\; ( \ln 2 - 0)= \dfrac{5}{2}\,\ln(2)= \ln(\sqrt{32}) \approx 1.73287
	\] \pvspace{1.3cm}

\checkin{01/20} We should integrate $\ds\int x e^{x^2} \;dx$ using integration-by-parts. \pspace

\sol The statement is \textit{false}. This certainly looks like an integration-by-parts integral---specifically, a tabular integral (because it is of the form polynomial times trig). The only possible choices for $dv$ would be $e^{x^2}$, which cannot be integrated, or $x e^{x^2}$, which is the entire integral and we already `don't know' how to integrate, or $x$. This last choices forces $u= e^{x^2}$. But then the new integral produced is $\ds\int x^3 e^{x^2} \;dx$---which is even `worse.' Instead, observe that the given integral can be found by $u$-substitution using $u= x^2$, so that $du= 2x \;dx$. But then\dots
	\[
	\int x e^{x^2} \;dx= \dfrac{1}{2} \int 2x e^{x^2} \;dx= \dfrac{1}{2} \int e^u \;du= \dfrac{e^u}{2} + C= \dfrac{e^{x^2}}{2} + C
	\]
There is a heavy overlap between $u$-substitution and integration-by-parts in terms of what the integrals that require these methods `look like.' One needs to be careful when determining when to use which method. Some integrals require both methods! \pvspace{1.3cm}

\sol The statement is \textit{false}. Recall that Heaviside's method (the cover-up method) only allows one to find the coefficient associated with the highest power of linear terms. % 02/19
\checkin{02/19} Because $\ds\lim_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{1}{n \ln(n)}= 0$, the sequence $\ds\sum_{n=2}^\infty \dfrac{1}{n \ln(n)}$ converges. \pspace

\sol The statement is \textit{false}. In fact, the given series diverges. One is making an incorrect inference from the Divergence Test. It is true that if $\ds\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n \neq 0$, then $\ds\sum^\infty a_n$ diverges---this is the Divergence Test. However, the Divergence Test does not say that if $\ds\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0$ that the series $\ds\sum^\infty a_n$ converges. One cannot determine whether a series $\ds\sum^\infty a_n$ converges or diverges from the fact that $\ds\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0$. Consider the series $\ds\sum^\infty \frac{1}{n}$ (the Harmonic series) and $\ds\sum^\infty \frac{1}{n^2}$. For both series, $\ds\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n}= 0$ and $\ds\lim{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n^2}= 0$. However, the series $\ds\sum^\infty \frac{1}{n}$ diverges (it is the Harmonic series), while the series $\ds\sum^\infty \frac{1}{n^2}$ converges to $\frac{\pi^2}{6}$. So, while $\ds\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n= 0$ in both cases, the series have different behaviors. The fact that $\ds\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n= 0$ is never enough to determine the behavior of $\ds\sum^\infty a_n$ without more information about the sequence $\{ a_n \}$. \pvspace{1.3cm}

\checkin{02/24} The series $\ds\sum_{n=2}^\infty 4 \left( \dfrac{9^{n+4}}{5^{2n}} \right)$ diverges. \pspace

\sol The statement is \textit{false}. Given a geometric series, one needs to be sure to write it in the form $\ds\sum^\infty a r^n$. That is, you need to find a common power $n$ for all the exponential terms. For this series, we have\dots
	\[
	\sum_{n=2}^\infty 4 \left( \dfrac{9^{n+4}}{5^{2n}} \right)= \sum_{n=2}^\infty 4 \left( \dfrac{9^n 9^4}{(5^2)^n} \right)= \sum_{n=2}^\infty (4 \cdot 9^4) \left( \dfrac{9^n}{25^n} \right)= \sum_{n=2}^\infty 26244 \left( \dfrac{9}{25} \right)^n
	\]
Observe this series is geometric because it has the form $\ds\sum^\infty ar^n$, where $a= 26244$ and $r= \frac{9}{25}$. Therefore, the series converges by the Geometric Series Test because $|r|= \frac{9}{25} < 1$. In fact, the series converges to $\frac{\text{first term}}{1 - r}= \frac{2125764/625}{1 - \frac{9}{25}}= \frac{531441}{100} \approx 5314.41$. \pvspace{1.3cm}

That is, the remainder term is the `next term' of the Taylor series but with the derivative instead evaluated at some other value between $x$ and $c$---not the center! So for $f(x)= e^x$ and $T_3(x)$ (centered at $0$), the remainder term would be $R_3(x)= \frac{f^{(4)}(c)}{4!}\, (x - 0)^4= \frac{f^{(4)}(c)}{4!}\, x^4$. Because $f^{(n)}(x)= e^x$ for all $n$, we have $R_3(x)= \frac{e^c}{4!}\, x^4$. Notice if the fourth derivative \textit{had} been evaluated at the center $0$, we would have had $\frac{e^0}{4!}\, x^4= \frac{1}{4!}\, x^4= \frac{x^4}{4!}$, which is the exact next term of the Taylor series. \pvspace{1.3cm}
